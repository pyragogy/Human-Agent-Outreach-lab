# Human × Agent Outreach Lab

**A real-world co-design experiment about what humans, AI agents, and human–agent teams are actually good at when building relationships around an open project.**

This repository did not begin with a research grant, a roadmap, or a benchmark.

It began with a cold email.

Daniel contacted Fabrizio with a proposal to help `UnPeeragogy` reach the right communities. The message was thoughtful enough that Fabrizio replied. The conversation continued. Their AI agents entered the exchange. A small outreach proposal became a larger question:

> **What changes when the same real-world outreach problem is approached by a human, by an agentic system, and eventually by both together?**

We do not know the answer.

Good.

That is why this lab exists.

---

## Point 0

We are **not running the experiment yet**.

We are not fundraising yet.

We have not agreed the final research question, metrics, protocol, agent architecture, or even whether the initial comparison is the best design.

That is intentional.

This repository is the shared table where the experiment will be **co-designed before it is executed**.

For now, the group is deliberately small:

| Human | Brings |
|---|---|
| **Fabrizio** | Pyragogy, Peeragogy, UnPeeragogy, open research, human–AI orchestration |
| **Daniel** | outreach practice, community discovery, relationship-building, campaign experience |

And each human may work with an AI agent.

The agents can research, challenge assumptions, summarize, prototype and keep context.

**The humans remain responsible for relationships, commitments and decisions.**

---

## Why this is interesting

The obvious experiment would be:

> Human vs AI. Who gets better numbers?

We think that question is too small.

A human practitioner and an agent swarm may optimize for very different things.

An agentic system may be excellent at:

- mapping large community landscapes;
- finding patterns quickly;
- testing many message variants;
- maintaining consistent tracking;
- reducing repetitive research work;
- operating cheaply at scale.

A human may be excellent at:

- understanding when *not* to contact someone;
- reading ambiguity and context;
- building trust;
- changing direction for reasons that cannot be reduced to a metric;
- recognizing unexpected opportunities;
- turning a message into a relationship.

And a hybrid system may be better than either alone.

So the interesting outcome is not necessarily a winner.

It may be discovering **where each form of agency creates value, where it fails, and where the combination becomes qualitatively different**.

---

## The origin is already part of the experiment

This project exists because one human outreach message produced something that neither side planned.

```
cold outreach
      ↓
human attention
      ↓
conversation
      ↓
agent ↔ agent reflection
      ↓
new shared idea
      ↓
possible collaboration
```

That sequence matters.

The original campaign had a price.

The conversation produced a new project.

That does **not** prove that humans are better than agents.

But it gives us a real event worth examining.

## A possible experiment — not a commitment

One design currently on the table looks like this:

| Phase | Mode | Question |
|---|---|---|
| **0** | Co-design | What are we actually trying to learn? |
| **1** | Human-led | What does a human practitioner produce under the agreed constraints? |
| **2** | Agent-led | What does an agentic system produce under comparable constraints? |
| **3** | Hybrid | What happens when agents handle discovery and humans handle high-value relationships? |
| **4** | Reflection | What changed our assumptions? What actually mattered? |

A possible initial constraint set is:

- same real project;
- same 14-day window;
- same starting budget;
- same high-level objectives;
- shared metrics;
- freedom to use different methods.

But **nothing after Phase 0 is fixed**.

If co-design gives us a better experiment, we change it.

---

## What we may measure

Stars and impressions are easy to count.

They are not enough.

We are more interested in signals such as:

- meaningful conversations;
- people who actually try the project;
- contributor intent;
- useful criticism;
- follow-up;
- field reports;
- durable relationships;
- cost per meaningful interaction;
- human time;
- compute / API cost;
- response quality;
- unexpected opportunities;
- outcomes that neither side predicted.

We will define these terms together before treating them as metrics.

## The working culture

This project borrows from [Peeragogy](https://peeragogy.org/), is informed by [UnPeeragogy](https://unpeeragogy.pyragogy.org/), and sits inside the broader [Pyragogy](https://pyragogy.org/) experiment.

But we do not want to decorate the project with theory.

We want to **use the theory on ourselves**.

### Take it easy

This must not become another job.

No artificial urgency.  
No heroic all-nighters.  
No pressure to keep momentum for its own sake.

### Learning by doing

We do not need the perfect protocol before taking the first useful step.

Small actions create information.

Information changes the roadmap.

### Co-design means co-design

Fabrizio does not design the experiment and invite Daniel into it.

Daniel does not deliver a finished campaign and ask Pyragogy to observe it.

The shared space is built together.

### Human first

Agents can propose.

Agents can disagree.

Agents can synthesize.

Agents can automate.

**Humans decide what they commit to.**

### Failure is evidence

If an idea fails, we keep it.

If a metric turns out to be useless, we record why.

If the experiment becomes unfair, we redesign it.

If we discover that the original question was wrong, that may be the most useful result.

### Open does not mean exposed

The protocol, assumptions, aggregate results, costs and learnings can be open.

Private contacts, personal relationships, sensitive information and legitimate proprietary methods do not have to be.

We will define that boundary together.

## Peeragogy, applied to ourselves

A few patterns seem especially useful here:

**Specific Project**  
Start with one concrete thing we can actually do.

**Roadmap**  
Keep direction visible, but revisable.

**Heartbeat**  
Maintain enough rhythm to stay connected without creating obligation.

**Wrapper**  
Make it easy to understand what happened while someone was away. Our agents may be particularly useful here.

**Carrying Capacity**  
Respect the amount of attention the humans actually have.

**Assessment**  
Ask what changed because we worked together, not merely whether tasks were completed.

And from UnPeeragogy:

> Make assumptions visible. Preserve disagreement. Look for counterevidence. Record the fracture, not only the success.

---

## Our first real task

When Discussions are enabled, the first thread should be deliberately simple:

### **What are we actually trying to learn?**

Fabrizio answers.

Daniel answers.

Each agent independently compares the two positions.

The agents should identify:

1. genuine overlap;
2. unresolved differences;
3. hidden assumptions;
4. questions neither human asked;
5. a candidate shared research question.

Then the humans review the result.

No automatic consensus.

No agent gets to declare alignment on behalf of its human.

If we end with one shared question that both humans recognize as worth exploring, **Point 0 has worked**.

---

## An UnPeeragogy check

From time to time we will stop and ask only three questions:

> **What did we expect?**  
> **What actually happened?**  
> **What surprised us?**

That may become one of the most important datasets in the project.

## What this repo is *not*

- Not an AI-vs-human cage match.
- Not a marketing stunt disguised as research.
- Not a benchmark designed to manufacture a winner.
- Not an excuse to automate unsolicited spam.
- Not a commitment to publish private working methods.
- Not a promise that the experiment will work.

It is a small shared lab built around a real problem, real people and real uncertainty.
